The four-part request

Verb, format, voice, context. Name all four and what comes back is specific, checkable, and yours.

The test for any request: what did you have to know, decide, or judge before you could type it? If the answer is "nothing," the answer you get back will be generic, and it will be the same generic answer everyone else gets.

The four parts

The model will quietly invent anything you leave out. The invented version is always the generic one.

1. Verb: the thinking move

Name the move, not the product.

Strong: compare, prioritize, find, flag, rank, critique, evaluate, diagnose, rehearse

Weak: write, make, create, do

"Write" names a document, not a thought. "Summarize" and "explain" are borderline: often they mean "repeat it back to me."

2. Format: the shape you can check

A table, a ranked list with reasons, two columns, before and after, a one-page memo with a fixed outline.

A table with an empty cell shows a hole. A paragraph hides one.

Ask for a step in the work (the options, the flags, the outline) before the finished product.

3. Voice: whose eyes

A skeptical board member, a parent reading on a phone, a finance officer, a reporter, a first-time applicant, an auditor.

The model simulates a point of view; it doesn't hold the truth. Change one word and the answer changes (see below).

4. Context: what only you know

Audience, purpose, reading level, length, the budget, the policy, what's already decided, what you can't promise.

The part everyone skips, and the part that does the most work. This is where your judgment lives.

Change the voice, change the answer

Same request, same facts. Only the voice changes.

What to do with itNone of the three is the truth; each is a real interest in your community. Ask for two voices that disagree, then account for the difference yourself. That accounting is the judgment AI can't do for you.

Using it with a team or a class

Two standards worth holding

If you skip these steps, you're handing off the judgment, not just the typing.
If you can't name the four parts of your request, you're not ready to trust the answer.

In a classroom, the second one is a teachable, gradable standard, and a better rule than "don't use it."

Roll it out like anything you teach

  1. Build one request in front of the group, thinking out loud.
  2. Build one together.
  3. Everyone builds one on their own real work.
  4. Post the four parts where people will see them.

Same in a staff meeting as in a classroom.

Where it fits: the 4D framework

Delegation: decide what's worth handing over. A teacher keeps the struggle that is the learning; a leader keeps the decision.

Description: the four parts live here.

Discernment: check what came back for "polished but thin."

Diligence: use it openly; show the request next to the result.
